Transformations in 3D

3D Coordinate Systems

  • Right-handed coordinate system:
  • Left-handed coordinate system:
By convention, positive rotations in a right-handed coordinate system are such that, when looking from a positive axis towards the origin, a 90 degree counterclockwise rotation will transform one positive axis into the next. Thus, if we’re looking from the positive x direction: y goes to z; If from y: z goes to x; If from z: x goes to y; In the left-handed coordinate system the direction is reversed. Another common rule is the “thumb rule”: imagine that your thumb is the positive z-axis. Your fingers curl from the positive x-axis towards the positive y-axis. Right-handed coordinate system is used to define transformations, since this is the standard mathematical convention. However, beware that not all graphics texts use a right-handed system. In particular, camera centered coordinate systems are often left-handed, because it is convenient to think of the z (depth) axis as pointing away from the viewer, such that more distant objects have greater depth (z) values.

Dot and Cross Products

  • Dot product: projection
  • Cross product (3D): perpendicular to both, right-hand rule
  • v × u = −u × v; zero when parallel
Needed for Rodrigues' formula and for positioning the camera. The dot product measures how much of v lies along u: ‖u‖‖v‖cos θ; with u a unit vector it is the signed length of v's projection onto u (week 2). It is zero exactly when the vectors are perpendicular. The cross product exists only in 3D. u × v is perpendicular to both u and v; its direction follows the right-hand rule (fingers from u to v, the thumb gives u × v; in a right-handed coordinate system, x × y = z), and its length is ‖u‖‖v‖sin θ, the area of the parallelogram they span. So it vanishes when u and v are parallel, and swapping them flips it: v × u = −u × v. Written as a matrix, u × v = [u]× v, with [u]× skew-symmetric. Demo: the θ slider opens the angle; watch the projection (blue band) shrink and turn negative past 90°, and the cross product grow to its maximum at 90° (the parallelogram is then a rectangle) and shrink back to zero at 180°. The buttons swap the order. Drag the view to turn it (double-click resets). Two uses coming up: in Rodrigues' formula, for a unit axis k, k × v is v⊥ turned by 90° about k (same length), and (k·v)k is v's projection onto the axis; in positioning the camera, cross products build the camera's perpendicular axes. And the normal of a triangle is the cross product of two of its edges.

3D Shearing

  • Shearing:
  • Only x changes, in proportion to y and z
  • Shears of y and of z are defined similarly
  • A cube becomes a parallelepiped of equal volume
Think of the unit cube as a stack of thin layers: each layer slides in x, in proportion to its y and z, like a deck of cards pushed sideways. Edges along x keep their length, the square faces become parallelograms, and the cube becomes a parallelepiped, still anchored at the origin, with the same volume: the determinant is 1. Putting all six off-diagonal entries into one matrix gives a general linear map, not a shear: with a = c = 1 and the others 0, for example, the determinant is 0 and the cube collapses flat.

Euler Angles

  • Euler’s rotation theorem: any rotation or sequence of rotations of a rigid body about a fixed point is equivalent to a single rotation by a given angle θ about a fixed axis (called the Euler axis) that runs through the fixed point.
  • Euler angles: the orientation of an object is specified by rotation angles about the X, Y, Z axes (performed in this order)
  • How can we smoothly interpolate between two different orientations?
  • Euler angle interpolation is not intuitive!
  • Gimbal Lock may occur… (video)
The result of any sequence of rotations of a 3D object about a fixed point in space, may be equivalently achieved by a single rotation about a fixed axis that runs through the same point. Related result: any orientation of a 3D object can be achieved by starting with the object in some canonical pose, and applying 3 rotations, one about each axis (in some predetermined order). This means that any orientation can be represented using 3 angles. Suppose we have two orientations and we’d like to interpolate between them. Since each orientation is represented using 3 angles, it’s natural to try to interpolate the three angles. However, the results are not always intuitive. Complex numbers: one imaginary unit,
i2 = −1
Quaternions: three imaginary units,
i2 = j2 = k2 = ijk = −1
  • Composing rotations is multiplying quaternions; no gimbal lock
Quaternions are covered in recitation 3: the algebra, rotating a vector by q [0, v] q⁻¹, the rotation matrix, and slerp. What a quaternion is, starting from complex numbers. A complex number has one real and one imaginary part, with i² = −1, and multiplying by the unit complex number cos θ + i sin θ rotates the plane by θ (left). Quaternions have one real part and three imaginary units i, j, k, one for each axis of space, with i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1 (so ij = k but ji = −k: the product does not commute, just like rotations in 3D). So a quaternion is a real number plus a 3D vector. A rotation by ϕ about the unit axis u is the unit quaternion with real part cos(ϕ/2) and vector part sin(ϕ/2) u (right): the same axis and angle as in Rodrigues' formula, stored in four numbers. (The half angle is because a vector v is rotated by q v q⁻¹, which applies q twice: R3.) Successive rotations multiply. There is no gimbal lock, and interpolating between two unit quaternions (slerp, along the unit sphere in 4D) gives a smooth interpolation between orientations, which is why animation systems and game engines store orientations this way.

Every transformation so far moved points within one coordinate system. The same matrices also change coordinates between frames. A frame is an origin o and three orthonormal axes u, v, w (given in world coordinates). A point with coordinates (a, b, c) in the frame is p = o + a u + b v + c w in the world: the matrix whose columns are u, v, w and o. Going the other way is its inverse; since R = [u v w] is orthonormal, R⁻¹ = Rᵀ, so the inverse is cheap: rotate by Rᵀ after translating by −o, which is the matrix shown. Two instances we use all the time: the modeling transformation (an object's own frame placed in the world: frame to world) and the viewing transformation (the world seen from the camera's frame: world to frame), which is how we will build the camera next.

Projections

Planar Geometric Projections

  • A projection is formed by the intersection of certain lines (projectors) with a plane (the projection plane)
  • Projectors are lines from the center of projection through each point on object
  • Center of projection at infinity results in a parallel projection
  • A finite center of projection results in a perspective projection
These types of projections are called “planar geometric projections”. They are called planar, because the projection is onto a plane, rather than some curved surface, and geometric because the projectors are straight lines, rather than curves.

Non-Planar Projections

  • Planar geometric projections: straight projectors, onto a plane
  • Many useful projections are not:
  • Fisheye lenses: the rays are bent, the image is a disk
  • Maps of the Earth: onto a cylinder (or a cone), and not by projectors
Many projections are either nonplanar or non-geometric. They are called "planar" because the projection is onto a plane, rather than some curved surface, and "geometric" because the projectors are straight lines, rather than curves. The next two slides take one example each, in the same way: first onto a sphere of directions, then a chosen mapping from the sphere to the plane. The Fisheye lens, and some other wide angle lenses implement a non-geometric projection, because light rays are bent by such lenses. The familiar Mercator projection (Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator, 1569) projects the surface of the Earth onto a cylinder, so it is nonplanar.

Fisheye Projection

a perspective image cannot reach 180°
perspective
fisheye
Why a fisheye image is a circle. Project in two steps: first onto a hemisphere around the COP (all that is left of a point is its direction: the angle θ from the optical axis and the angle around it), then flatten the hemisphere onto the image plane. Perspective sends a direction at angle θ to distance f tan θ from the center, which runs off to infinity as θ approaches 90°: a plane can never hold a 180° field of view. The (equidistant) fisheye sends it to f θ, the length of the arc, unrolled onto the plane: at 90° that is f π/2, finite. The whole hemisphere fits in a disk, and its rim (the directions at 90° to the axis) is the circle. Demo: drag the projector on the left (double-click resets); the point where it hits the wall is marked in both views. Right: the room seen from its middle, computed per pixel. Switch to perspective and raise the field of view: the walls stretch without bound (perspective stops at 170° here). In the fisheye at 180°, the back wall is in the middle and the four side walls all around it, up to the plane of the camera. Step: two straight lines on the ceiling, one across the room and one along it. A line and the COP span a plane, which cuts the sphere in a great circle; a great circle maps to a curve in the fisheye image, unless that plane contains the optical axis: a line parallel to the axis (like the one along the ceiling, or the room's long edges) points at the image center and stays straight. In perspective, both are straight. Optional: other lens models put the same direction at other distances: equisolid angle 2f sin(θ/2), stereographic 2f tan(θ/2), orthographic f sin θ. Real lenses are close to one of these; calibration measures the actual r(θ).

Cylindrical Projections

The companion of the fisheye slide. Wrap a cylinder around the globe, touching it at the equator. Every map in this family unrolls longitude evenly, x = R λ, and differs only in the height y it gives latitude φ. Left: a meridian seen from the side; drag the point (double-click resets). All three maps are drawn at the same scale. Central cylindrical: the projector through the center of the Earth meets the cylinder at R tan φ. A true geometric projection, the analog of perspective (f tan θ): it runs off to infinity at the poles, fast. Equirectangular: the arc length R φ, unrolled, like the fisheye's f θ: the whole sphere fits in a 2:1 rectangle. Mercator: not a projection by projectors at all. At latitude φ the parallel (circumference 2πR cos φ) is stretched to the full width 2πR, by sec φ; Mercator stretches north-south by the same factor, dy/dφ = R sec φ, which integrates to y = R ln tan(π/4 + φ/2). The map is conformal: small shapes keep their angles. The Tissot circles are identical small circles on the globe. Mercator keeps them circles, but they grow toward the poles (conformal, not equal-area: Greenland looks as large as Africa, which is 14 times larger). Equirectangular stretches them sideways into ellipses; central cylindrical stretches them even more vertically. Step: a rhumb line, a route of constant compass bearing (65° east of north). It crosses every meridian at the same angle, so in the conformal Mercator map it is a straight line: why navigators used it. In the other two maps it curves. Visual computing connection: the equirectangular map is the standard format for 360° panoramas and environment maps (the whole sphere of directions in one rectangle), which come back in weeks 8 and 12. Non-linear (but monotonic) mapping of Z values

This plot shows the values of Z after the perspective transformation (for near = 1 and far = 10 by default; the sliders change them, on a logarithmic scale). The Z values in the interval [-1,-10] are mapped in a non-linear, but monotonic, fashion to the range [-1,1]. We will also see that this mapping preserves lines and planes. This property along with the monotonicity allows us to subsequently use the Z values for depth comparisons which we need to make in order to perform hidden surface removal later on. The near plane matters more than the far one. z_ndc = (f+n)/(f−n) − 2fn/((f−n)d), so half of the [−1, 1] range is used up by d = 2nf/(f+n), which is about 2n when f ≫ n: the precision is spent close to the eye. Try n = 0.1, f = 1000: half of the range is gone by d ≈ 0.2. The resolution of a 24-bit depth buffer at distance d is about 2⁻²⁴ (f−n) d²/(fn): at the far plane, 2⁻²⁴ (f−n) f/n, which grows like f/n. Pushing the far plane out costs little; pulling the near plane in costs a lot. Far-away surfaces whose depths fall into the same bucket flicker (z-fighting), back in week 7.

View Frustum Clipping

  • After applying the projection and normalizing, all points inside the view frustum lie in the canonical cube [−1,1]×[−1,1]×[−1,1]
The coordinates of any point after the perspective divide must lie inside the [-1,1] cube. Thus, in homogeneous coordinates it must hold that x/w < 1 and also x/w > -1, and so forth for the y and z coordinates. Note that points in front of the eye all have a negative z coordinate, since the camera is looking down the negative Z axis. Thus, their w coordinate must be positive. Then multiplying the inequalities by w does not flip the inequality, and we get x < w, x > -w, and so forth. Clipping in homogeneous coordinates has two main advantages: The sign of w is still known, so primitives that cross the eye plane (w = 0) can be clipped correctly. After the perspective divide the sign is lost: a point behind the eye can get valid-looking screen coordinates x and y, mirrored (its z_ndc is always greater than 1, so a single point is still rejected), and an edge or triangle with vertices on both sides of the eye plane, drawn between its projected vertices, covers the wrong part of the screen; The clipping algorithm is exactly the same regardless of the type of projection: orthographic or perspective. Summary: Coordinate Systems

Positioning the objects and placing and pointing the camera is composited into a single transformation matrix that transforms object coordinates into eye coordinates (sometimes referred to as modelview) The projection matrix transforms eye coordinates into coordinates in the canonical or normalized view volume, where they can be clipped efficiently. The clipped coordinates can now be projected onto the projection plane, yielding normalized device coordinates. Note, however, that we still keep the z coordinate around, in order to facilitate hidden line and hidden surface removal when scan-converting. The viewport transformation takes the normalized device coordinates, which are typically in the range [-1,1] in x and y. and transforms them to raster coordinates, e.g., [0.0, 640.0] and [0.0, 480.0]. The z coordinate is transformed to the [0,1] range for hidden surface removal purposes.

The Camera Matrix in Computer Vision

  • From a 3D point to a pixel, in two steps:
  • [R | t], the extrinsics: where the camera is and where it points (the view matrix)
  • K, the intrinsics: the lens and the sensor
f: the focal length, in pixels (the zoom)
(cx, cy): the image center
one 3 × 4 matrix takes a 3D point to its pixel
The graphics pipeline without depth:
graphics keeps z only for the z-buffer
Computer vision describes the same camera with a different vocabulary. A world point X reaches a pixel in two steps. First, [R | t] expresses the point in the camera's own frame: the rotation R and the translation t say where the camera is and where it points. This is exactly the view matrix (its top three rows), and these are called the extrinsic parameters. Second, K, the intrinsic matrix, describes the lens and the sensor: f is the focal length measured in pixels (zooming in increases it), and (c_x, c_y) is the image center, the pixel where the optical axis meets the image. In the figure, the ray from X to the camera center crosses the image plane at the pixel (x, y). Together, P = K [R | t] is a 3 × 4 matrix that takes a 3D point (in homogeneous coordinates) to its pixel (divide by the third coordinate, as always). It has 11 degrees of freedom: 6 for the pose (3 for the rotation, 3 for the position) and 5 for K in general (the two focal lengths may differ if the pixels are not square, and a skew term, which is zero for real sensors). The connection with the graphics pipeline: multiply the viewport transformation by the perspective projection matrix of a symmetric frustum and apply it to eye coordinates. The x, y and w rows are the camera matrix (f = (W/2) cot(θ/2) for an image W pixels wide with field of view θ; the image center is the middle of the viewport); the remaining row computes depth, which graphics keeps for the z-buffer and a camera does not need. The two fields use different conventions (graphics: the camera looks down −z and image y points up; vision: the camera looks down +z and image y points down); recitation 3 goes through them.

Camera Calibration

  • Known: 3D points on an object, and their pixels in a photo
  • Unknown: the camera, P = K [R | t]
  • Each point gives two equations (its x and y)
  • P has 11 unknowns: 6 points are enough
  • more points: the best fit (least squares)
  • Then split P into K (the lens) and R, t (where the camera is)
Used for measuring from photographs, augmented reality, and inserting synthetic objects into photos
Calibration runs the camera backwards: from points whose 3D positions are known (the corners of a calibration object, here two checkerboards at a right angle) and the pixels where they appear in a photograph, recover the camera matrix P, and from it the camera's intrinsics and pose. Each point gives two equations, one for its image x coordinate and one for its y, and both are linear in the 12 entries of P. Since P is only defined up to scale, it has 11 unknowns, so 6 points suffice (they must not all lie on one plane: a single plane does not determine the camera). In practice there are many more points, and the measured pixels are noisy, so we take the P that fits all the equations best (least squares). P = K [R | t], with K upper triangular and R a rotation, and the two can be pulled apart again: this gives the focal length and image center, and where the camera was and where it pointed. The details are in recitation 3. Real lenses also bend straight lines a little (lens distortion); practical calibration tools (for example OpenCV's) use several photographs of a flat checkerboard and estimate the distortion too, starting from a linear estimate like this one. We use calibration in week 12 to insert a synthetic object into a real photograph.
