Geometric Transformations

  • Why do we need them?
  • Want to define an object in one coordinate system, then place it in another system.
  • Allow us to create multiple instances of objects.
  • Animation (time-dependent transformations).
  • Display using device independent coordinates.
  • 3D viewing (projections).
Motivation: modeling (local to world coordinates), instancing, animation, device-independent display, and, in week 3, 3D viewing.

Basic Geometric Entities

  • Scalars - real numbers
  • sizes/lengths, angles, parameters
  • Vectors - typically 2D, 3D, 4D
  • directions
  • Points - typically 2D, 3D, 4D
  • positions
  • Vectors and Points - same representation, different semantics
  • An affine space: a vector space plus a set of points, with
  • point + vector = point, and point − point = vector
Most of the work in Computer Graphics involves these three types of geometric entities. Although it might seem that vectors and points are identical, since both may be represented as an n-tuple of real numbers, their geometric meaning is very different, and the sets of operations that make sense on vectors and points are different as well, as we shall see in the following slides. Let V be a vector space and P be a set of points. Together they define an affine space A: A contains P, and adding any vector in V to any point in P results in another point in P. Also, the difference between any two points in P is a vector in V. No distinguished origin: “all frames are equal”. Still no notion of distances or angles (those come from the dot product, next slide).

Operations on Vectors

  • Addition
  • Multiplication by a scalar
  • Dot product
  • Length (norm)
  • Unit vector (pure direction)
  • Cartesian coordinates: a standard orthonormal basis, of pairwise perpendicular unit vectors
Note: our convention in the course is that vectors are column vectors. In some texts, you will see the vectors written as row vectors. In the Euclidean space we use the dot-product metric to define the length/size of a vector, and it’s convenient to think of a general vector as size*pure direction, where the pure direction is given by the vector normalized to unit norm. The dot product is also used to define the angle between two vectors. Cartesian space: the basis of the vector space (chosen for the frame) consists of unit length orthogonal vectors. These are what rigid and similarity transformations preserve (lengths and angles, later in this lecture), and why a rotation matrix is orthonormal: its columns are rotated basis vectors.

Operations on Points

  • Can’t simply add points to each other…
  • but can add a vector to a point
  • and can subtract points
  • Can’t multiply a point by a scalar…
  • but can take an affine combination of points:
  • for all t: the line through a and b
Let V be a vector space and P be a set of points. Together they define an affine space A: A contains P, and adding any vector in V to any point in P results in another point in P. Also, the difference between any two points in P is a vector in V. We can add vectors to points to obtain other points since this is how an affine space is defined. We can take the difference between two points obtaining a vector, again from the definition of an affine space. Adding points or scalar-point multiplication is not defined in an affine space. However a linear combination of points c = sa + tb with weights s+t=1 is really an affine combination: sa + tb = (1 – t)a + tb = a + t(b – a). Therefore, it does have a meaning (yields another point in the space). The line through a and b is the set of all their affine combinations, L(t) = a + t(b − a): the same representation in any dimension. Affine transformations preserve affine combinations (later in this lecture), so they map lines to lines. Next: three points, and a plane.   • and can subtract points

  • for all α, β, γ: the plane through a, b and c
  • For n points, Σαipi is a point if Σαi = 1,

The same slide, with three points. An affine combination of a, b and c, with weights α + β + γ = 1, is again a point: p = a + β(b − a) + γ(c − a), the point a plus a combination of two vectors. Taking all such weights gives the plane through a, b and c (here, the whole 2D plane), just as two points gave a line. Again the same representation in any dimension: in 3D, this is how a plane is spanned by three points. By extension, combination of many points (n > 2) are also allowed. Because all weights sum to 1, we can express the first one as (1-a1-a2…-an) Actually, we can also take a combination where the weights sum to 0. This is called a “vector combination” and results in a vector. Proof is left as an exercise. Things to show with the demo: p is inside the (shaded) triangle exactly when all three weights are positive; on the line through two of the points, the third weight is 0; at a vertex, its weight is 1. (For you: these are barycentric coordinates. They are not named here; they return in week 7, for interpolating across triangles.) Transformations in 2D

  • We can think of a geometric object as a set of points:
  • Boundary representation: the points form the boundary of the object.
  • Solid representation: the points form the interior of the object.
  • Question: how do we transform a set of points in the plane?
Since objects are represented by their point sets, the transformations we are interested in are simply mappings that specify for each point in the plane where it should move. Many kinds of such transformations are possible. The most commonly used transformations in graphics are called affine transformations.

Translation

  • Translate(a,b):
Do we transform the object, or the coordinate system?
One way to think about the translate transformation is: a mapping which maps each point (x,y) a units in the x direction and b units in the y direction. Another way to think about it is: a change of coordinate system, which changes the current coordinate system to a new coordinate system, whose origin is at (-a,-b), and with the new axes parallel to the old ones. The Projective Plane

  • A point in the projective plane P2 is represented by 3 coordinates, at least one of which is non-zero.
  • Two 3-vectors a,b represent the same point in P2 iff a = hb, where h is a non-zero scalar.
  • A 2D point (x, y) in the Euclidean plane corresponds to all of the 3-vectors (hx,hy,h) in P2, such as (x,y,1).
    Note: this is a one-to-many correspondence!
  • Geometric interpretation: each point (x,y) corresponds to a ray in 3D, from the origin (0,0,0) through the point (x,y,1)
Projective spaces: A point of an n-dimensional projective space, P^n, is represented by an n+1 vector of coordinates, where at least of coordinate is non zero.These coordinates are called the homogeneous or projective coordinates of the point. Two n+1 vectors a,b represent the same point iff there exists a nonzero scalar λ, such that λa = b.Thus, the correspondence between points and homogeneous coordinates is one to many. Theorem: The space P^m is topologically equivalent to the unit sphere S^m of R^(m+1) in which we have identified antipodal points. P^1 - the projective line. Topologically equivalent to a unit circle. P^2 - the projective plane. Topologically equivalent to a sphere. Principle of duality: there is no difference between points and lines in P^2. A point can be thought of as the set of all lines passing through it. A line can be thought of as the set of all points on it. Let x be the coordinates of the point, and u be the coefficients of the line equation. The equation uTx = 0 defines the set of lines through a point, and also a set of points on a line. (This is too complicated. Stolfi had a more intuitive explanation).

The Projective Plane

Geometrically, we can think of points with a homogeneous coordinate as 3D points that live in a special space: the projective plane. Let’s embed the 2D plane in a 3D space, by adding a third axis, which we will call W. Now, all of the 2D points will in fact live on the W=1 plane, so a location (x,y) will actually be represented by the three coordinates (x,y,1) Furthermore, in the projective plane, all the points of the form (hx, hy, h), i.e. on the ray that goes from the origin through (x,y,1) are considered equivalent, corresponding to the same 2D point (x,y). h must be non-zero. The Projective Plane

  • A 2D vector (x, y) in the Euclidean plane corresponds to all of the 3-vectors (hx, hy, 0) in P2, such as (x, y, 0).
  • A 3-vector whose 3rd coordinate is 0 has two geometric interpretations (which are really the same):
  • A direction
  • A point at infinity

Affine Transformations: Definition

  • Let T : A1 → A2, where A1 and A2 are affine spaces.
  • Then T is said to be an affine transformation if:
  • T maps vectors to vectors and points to points
  • T is a linear transformation on vectors
  • If p is a point and u is a vector:
    T(p + u) = T(p) + T(u)
Affine transformations map vectors to vectors and points to points. They are linear transformations on vectors but not on points (because of translation). They preserve affine combinations. Linear: T(aA + bB) = aT(A) + bT(B) Any linear transformation is an affine one, but not vice versa. Thus affine transformations are a superclass of linear ones.

Affine Transformations: Properties

  • Affine transformations preserve affine combinations of points. In other words, given an affine transformation T and a point p:
  • it holds that:
  • Intersections between lines are preserved.
  • Parallel lines are preserved.
The first property is very important, it means among other things that lines map onto lines under affine transformations (similarly, planes map onto planes). Furthermore, given a line defined as an affine combination of two points a, b, the affine image of this line is defined by the images of these two points: T(a), T(b). Another consequence of this property is that parallel lines remain parallel after an affine transformation. If this was not the case, there would be a mutual point on two lines after an affine transformation while there were none before. The mutual point may be expressed as an affine combination of the endpoints of both lines. Since affine combinations are preserved, this means the point must have been mutual already before the transformation: a contradiction. A related consequence is that intersection points are preserved by affine transformation. So we either compute an intersection point and then transform it, or transform the lines first and then compute the intersection – the result will be the same.

Cubic Interpolation

  • Between samples k and k + 1: a cubic p(t) with
  • Four conditions for the four coefficients of a cubic
  • Passes through the samples
  • The slope at each sample is set by its two neighbors, so the curve is smooth

No convolution here: a cubic between each two samples, determined by four conditions. For a time t: the intermediate mesh M_t, vertex by vertex; A warped from M_A to M_t; B warped from M_B to M_t (the tildes); and the morph, their cross-dissolve. At t = 0 the morph is A, at t = 1 it is B. The warps are drawn with the "same (a, b) in corresponding cells" rule; triangles would serve as well. Demo: move t with the slider (steps of 0.1). Drag interior vertices of either mesh to align features (head outline, chin, mouth, ears) and watch the ghosting disappear; with both meshes regular the morph is a plain cross-dissolve. The default meshes sit on corresponding features (hairline, eyes, mouth, chin), as in Wolberg's figure. Double-click a mesh to reset both. Photos: two generated portraits (no real people), used as they are (no cropping or alignment).
